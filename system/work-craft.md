# Doing good work in an organization: questions for the moment of judgment

> This is a draft. It has not yet passed a blind test on real decisions, so read it as a hypothesis until the questions are shown to actually change judgments.

**How to use it.** This is not a rule list. At each of three moments, ask one bundled question and expand only where the answer gets stuck. Use the same questions when helping a person with their work and when an agent writes its own report. Each bundle's "when it becomes a score" names the distortion that appears when the question itself becomes the target.

## Skeleton: two ledgers

- **Inside ledger, flow.** The value your work gave the organization: decisions changed, incidents prevented, service levels kept, obligations closed.
- **Inside ledger, stock.** Delegated trust: how far the organization hands you judgment and resources without asking, after seeing that flow repeatedly. It builds slowly and drops fast when one large promise staked on someone else's numbers turns out wrong.
- **Outside ledger.** Two stocks that survive a change of organization: capability (it stays in your head and hands, but others cannot see it) and evidence (artifacts a stranger can check, results with dates and numbers, people who will vouch with their own reputation).
- **Conversion.** Turning flow into stock. Flow that the evaluator sees becomes trust; capability accumulates while you work; evidence exists only if you leave it deliberately.
- **Where conversion ends.** Not when the output ships, but when the promised downstream state is realized. Half-built work, work that was only connected, and agreements that were only spoken barely enter either ledger.

## Three moments

### 1. Before sending or recording

**Bundled question.** Who must do what with this, and which claim can be checked against what?

- What must the recipient do after reading? Could someone seeing it for the first time restate it? → Bring a decision with one recommendation attached, put settled items one line each, and put the one sentence to be passed on first.
- What has the evaluator actually seen, and what one unseen thing should be shown? → One line in the record or one demo.
- Could a stranger check this sentence? Is it "moved together" or "changed it and it differed"? → Attach a number, a date, a comparison, or lower the claim.
- **Safe to skip when.** It is a notice with nothing for the recipient to do or pass on, a pure factual record that claims no cause or credit, and not an input to evaluation, resource allocation, or a stop decision. If you always review the output together with the evaluator, ask the second question lightly. Sharing a room alone does not switch it off. Ask the first question once, since you need it to tell whether this is only a notice.
- **When it becomes a score.** If an easily restated sentence becomes the goal, conditions and uncertainty get cut into slogans. If visible volume becomes the goal, demos and report counts grow instead of value. If only quickly checkable numbers count as results, long research, prevention, and maintenance disappear.

### 2. Before deciding or promising

**Bundled question.** Who decides, who pays if it is wrong, and can it be undone?

- Does the other side have a reason to decide this differently from you? Can it be undone? → If there is a reason to differ or it cannot be undone, bring it first with a recommendation. Otherwise do it and leave one line afterward. That line is cheap insurance for a boundary drawn in the wrong place.
- If it is wrong, whose numbers, time, or reputation take the hit? → If they are someone else's, keep the promise small (the next checkpoint date instead of the result, the state you will look at that day, the condition for folding) and the exploration large. If they are all yours, you can bet big.
- Where did the power that made this work come from, and does the result survive if that power disappears tomorrow? → Write the decision down, attach names and numbers to the result, and name who takes it over.
- **Safe to skip when.** The first question only when the boundary is agreed in writing and still valid. The second only when the cost is entirely your own time (switch it back on if that time has an opportunity cost or a hidden dependency). Never skip the third. You have to ask it to know whether expertise is your only source of power.
- **When it becomes a score.** Choosing only promises with no downside builds trust but changes nothing. Bringing everything to others first looks safe but eats their time.

### 3. When choosing work or opening a new branch

**Bundled question.** Which organizational results and obligations must be protected, and within that, what capability and evidence can you leave behind?

- Does this work hold the floor (legal deadlines, outages, promised service levels)? → Choose by the selection rule below.
- If you lack the authority to choose, what can you still choose? → The scope, what you will learn, and the shape of the record you leave.
- Is the added gain in capability and evidence still zero even where you could have chosen? → Your goal may be drifting toward the organization's scorecard. Write one sentence on what the organization gains and what you gain this period, and set a date to ask again.
- **Safe to skip when.** Never. Even when circumstances remove the options, the narrow board (scope, learning, record) remains.
- **When it becomes a score.** Counting cells as a score makes you avoid confidential work that leaves no evidence, or mandatory work where one cell is worth a great deal.

## Selection rule: the floor first

1. **The floor first.** Pass the floor of organizational results and obligations.
2. **Weight within it.** Compare the remaining options by the added gain in capability and evidence. Do not count cells.
3. **Size beats cell count.** Work that only fills organizational value can be central if its value is overwhelming. Work with zero organizational value is not the center of your working hours, however much capability and evidence it adds.

A made-up example: A has organizational value 100 with capability and evidence 0; B has 2 in all three. Counting cells picks B, but if A prevents a legal deadline miss or a major outage, A comes first. Added gain is an estimate, and which work was the lever often shows only later.

## Seven hidden causes (what it is / what you see when it is off)

Invisible variables that produce several surface judgments at once. Hypotheses about "why it turns out that way", not rules.

- **Delegation boundary and forecasting.** How much the line you decide alone overlaps with the other side's line, and how well your stated dates and risks come true / "Why didn't you tell me" and "Why bring this to me" alternate, and bad news surfaces late.
- **Observation bandwidth.** How much of your value flow the evaluator actually sees / You did a lot, but evaluation follows a few visible events and first impressions, and the work of unblocking others vanishes.
- **Attribution of loss.** Who pays when it is wrong, and how much trust you have built with that person / A big promise staked on someone else's numbers breaks, or the opposite: your schedule is full, you stake nothing, and you only do work others decided.
- **Source and lifetime of influence.** Where the power that gets things done comes from and when it drops to zero / Things work only through borrowed power and relationships, and when that person leaves, past decisions reopen.
- **Author of the goal.** Who wrote the goal you are chasing and when you will ask again / The sentence describing your goal has turned, undated, into the language of the organization's scorecard.
- **Cost of faking the signal.** Whether a stranger evaluating you can check the evidence / Only a title and "led" remain, with no artifact to open, no number, and nobody to vouch.
- **Power to close at the boundary.** The power to carry work past the boundary between teams and roles all the way to the promised result state / Lots of analysis and models, but neither decisions nor states change; success belongs to everyone and failure to no one.

## Signs this document is wrong

If using these questions on real decisions changes no judgment compared with not using them, drop the two ledgers and the hidden causes and keep only the questions.
